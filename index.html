🌙 LunarLook – Lunar Landing Site Scout

Compare up to three landing sites and dates. See where the Sun and Earth sit against the horizon, how long until sunset or Earthset, and when a lander has both power and a direct-to-Earth link.

New here? Why is the Moon’s south pole so hard?
  • The Moon has no atmosphere, so there is no twilight. The Sun is either above the horizon or it is dark and about −170 °C.
  • Near the pole the Sun skims the horizon and circles it once per lunar day (~29.5 Earth days). Hills and crater rims decide whether a lander is lit.
  • Earth never rises far above the horizon at the pole, and the Moon wobbles (libration), so a direct radio link can come and go. A few degrees of terrain can cut communication time sharply.
  • Moving a landing by a few km or a few days can swing power and comms windows a lot. That is why fast comparison matters.

Method: Sun and Earth directions come from truncated analytical lunar and solar ephemerides plus optical libration (Meeus-style formulas), accurate to roughly a fraction of a degree – good for scouting, not for flight design. Earth is treated as a point (center must clear the horizon); the Sun uses its upper limb. The terrain horizon is a synthetic ridge profile scaled by the slider; real use should load LOLA-derived horizon masks and verify with JPL Horizons or Quickmap. Azimuth: 0° = lunar north, 90° = east (at the pole it is relative to the 0° meridian).
